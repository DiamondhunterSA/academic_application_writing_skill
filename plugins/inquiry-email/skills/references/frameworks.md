# Inquiry Email Frameworks

Use these details within the shared Gather → Plan → Write → Polish → Iterate workflow. Select what the current inquiry needs rather than turning every email into a full application statement.

## Recipient and Purpose

Identify the decision or information the sender wants, then choose a recipient who can reasonably provide it.

| Recipient | Suitable Focus | Distinction to Preserve |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------------|
| Prospective supervisor / principal investigator | Research alignment, supervision availability, relevant opportunities | A research match does not establish that the recipient is recruiting, has funding, or can make an admission decision alone. |
| Research group / project contact | RA, research internship, visiting placement, project participation | Match the inquiry to the advertised role or actual project, including practical availability. |
| Admissions / program office | Eligibility, required materials, application procedures, official timelines | Ask about rules or unresolved cases; do not ask an administrator to assess research fit as a supervisor would. |
| Funding / scholarship office | Award eligibility, funding routes, conditions, application procedures | Distinguish an award's eligibility from the chance of receiving it and from admission to the program. |
| Employer / research collaborator | A relevant opening, possible contribution, shared research question | An inquiry explores an opportunity; follow any required formal application or proposal channel. |

When the correct contact is uncertain, ask the appropriate office for direction or draft a short routing question. Do not assume that the most senior person is the best recipient.

## Context Worth Gathering

Use existing material first. Gather only details that affect this email:

- **Sender:** Current degree or role, the name or affiliation the sender wants to use, and relevant experience when needed for the chosen direction or question.
- **Opportunity:** Program, position, project, collaboration, or policy being asked about.
- **Timing:** Intended intake, start date, duration, availability, or an actual deadline where relevant.
- **Connection, when needed:** A verified research topic, project, publication, advertised requirement, prior meeting, or shared contact.
- **Desired response:** The specific clarification or next step the recipient can provide.
- **Direction preference:** The user's requested approach or an applicable, previously confirmed preference. For PhD supervision inquiries, use the definitions and selection guidance in [formats.md](formats.md#1-initial-contact-with-a-prospective-supervisor).
- **Thread and files:** Earlier correspondence, any instructions already received, and files the user actually wants to attach.

A full CV, transcript, life story, or detailed research proposal is not necessary for every inquiry. If an essential detail is missing, ask for it; otherwise omit it rather than filling the gap with a plausible claim.

## Known Facts and Open Questions

Separate facts supplied by the user, information checked against a source, and questions still unresolved. Do not turn an open question into a statement about availability, funding, eligibility, or likely acceptance.

Use official program pages, current group pages, vacancy notices, or the relevant publication when verification is needed and available. Keep source notes outside the email unless a link or exact reference helps the recipient identify the issue. An old announcement or faculty listing alone does not establish a current opening.

Describe the sender's contact with research accurately. Reading an abstract is not reading the full paper; seeing a project summary is not studying the methods. Name a publication only when its identity and the connection are supported, and do not claim to have reproduced results, followed a career, or read extensive work without that basis.

If information cannot be verified, frame it as a question or omit it. Put unresolved drafting issues in a separate note rather than hiding uncertainty in a confident sentence.

## Email Components

### Subject

Make the topic recognizable using the opportunity, research area, intake, or role that matters. For example, a supervision inquiry may identify the intended intake, while an administrative inquiry may identify the program and the requirement in question.

Avoid exaggerated urgency, praise, and vague subjects such as "A question." Continue an existing thread when the topic is unchanged instead of introducing a new subject for every reply.

### Opening and Identification

State the reason for writing early and introduce only the background needed to understand it. Use the recipient's name and title when known; an office or team salutation is appropriate for a shared address. Do not guess credentials or invent a prior connection.

A prior meeting, referral, or earlier reply can supply useful context when it actually occurred. Make the connection explicit enough to recognize without suggesting a closer relationship than exists.

### Evidence of Relevance

Include evidence of a connection when the selected direction or specific question needs it. Choose the strongest supported link between the sender's experience or question and the recipient's work or responsibility, and explain it briefly rather than listing technical skills, publications, or compliments. A factual identity and clear question may be sufficient without a project account or fit argument.

For research contacts, a specific problem, method, or result can show alignment if supported by the material. For administrative contacts, the relevant evidence may instead be the exact requirement, qualification, or conflicting instruction. Do not add faculty praise to a procedural question.

### A Clear, Low-Burden Ask

Make the main question answerable: whether supervision might be available, whether a qualification meets a stated rule, which funding route applies, or whether a proposed contribution is relevant. Add related details only when needed to answer that question.

Avoid bundling unrelated requests or asking the recipient to review an entire application when a narrower clarification would suffice. A meeting or detailed discussion can be offered as a next step when useful, without assuming the recipient will agree or demanding a time commitment.

### Closing

Close with brief thanks and an appropriate name or signature. Include relevant contact details if they are not already clear from the channel. Enthusiasm should come from the substance of the inquiry, not flattery, pleading, or promises of papers, unpaid work, or other commitments the user did not provide.

## Attachments and References

Say a file is attached only when it has been supplied or the user confirms it is available and intends to include it. Use an accurate description of the file; do not invent a CV, transcript, proposal, portfolio, or attachment filename.

If no attachment is ready, omit the claim or offer to provide material later when appropriate. Follow the recipient's stated instructions about attachments or application portals. A link must point to the intended page or document; a draft placeholder is not a verified link.

## Length and Information Selection

Use enough space to identify the sender and ask the question clearly, including background or research fit as needed for the chosen direction or question. Follow any explicit limit; do not impose the same word count on a first contact, an eligibility question, and a brief reply.

Keep information that changes the recipient's ability to answer. Remove repeated CV content, generic institutional praise, background already visible in the thread, and explanations unrelated to the request. Preserve essential dates, conditions, and distinctions when shortening.

## Thread Continuity

Read the latest reply and relevant earlier messages before drafting. Keep names, opportunity titles, dates, files, and commitments consistent with that thread. Answer questions the recipient has already asked rather than sending the original introduction again.

Check actual dates, stated timelines, deadlines, and any follow-up policy before suggesting another message. A follow-up is not a reason to invent urgency or presume that silence means rejection. A rejection or request for no further contact ends pursuit of that opportunity unless the recipient explicitly invites a different next step.
