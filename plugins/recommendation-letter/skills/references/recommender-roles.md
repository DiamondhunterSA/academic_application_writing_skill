# Recommender Roles

Use the recommender's actual relationship to choose evidence and the scope of the evaluation. Combined roles can support a letter that draws on several settings. Explain each setting when moving between examples.

## Project Supervisor

### Relationship and Evidence

Establish the project's setting, the supervision period, the applicant's assigned responsibility, and the supervisor's involvement. Relevant settings include capstone projects, engineering development, design competitions, collaborative software work, and applied investigations.

Useful evidence includes design reviews, implementation choices, prototype demonstrations, testing records, documentation, coordination problems, and responses to feedback. Select an episode in which the supervisor could assess the applicant's decisions. Explain the applicant's contribution within the team outcome.

### Evaluation and Voice

A project supervisor can assess practical judgment, technical ownership, execution, reliability, collaboration, and improvement under constraints. Connect these observations to academic readiness where supported.

Use concrete language about decisions, responsibilities, and results. Explain why a tradeoff was reasonable or how the applicant addressed a setback. Preserve the actual scale of leadership and independence.

### Paragraph Pattern

> During [project], I supervised [applicant]'s work on [responsibility]. When [specific difficulty] arose, [applicant] [documented action]. The resulting [test, prototype, or deliverable] showed [supported outcome]. This episode gave me confidence in [applicant]'s ability to [evaluative conclusion relevant to the application].

## Research Supervisor

### Relationship and Evidence

Establish the research setting, period of supervision, project question, and form of interaction when supplied. Preserve the recommender's actual role, such as principal investigator, thesis supervisor, co-supervisor, or day-to-day research mentor.

Useful evidence includes research discussions, literature synthesis, experimental design, controls, analytical decisions, lab notebooks, code reviews, interpretations of unexpected results, and revisions to a manuscript or presentation. Specify the applicant's intellectual and practical contributions. Maintain supplied distinctions among preliminary findings, submitted manuscripts, accepted papers, and published work.

### Evaluation and Voice

A research supervisor can assess curiosity, methodological judgment, independence, persistence, critical reasoning, response to uncertainty, and scholarly communication. Show development through a documented change in reasoning or responsibility.

Use an analytical voice that explains what the applicant understood and how the supervisor assessed it. A specific response to inconclusive evidence can demonstrate research potential. Calibrate predictions about future work to the applicant's experience.

### Paragraph Pattern

> In our work on [research question], [applicant] identified [specific issue] in [analysis or method]. Through [supplied action], [applicant] established [supported finding or limitation] and proposed [documented next step]. Our discussions of this work showed me [applicant]'s capacity to [research judgment]. I consider this preparation valuable for [relevant form of graduate research].

## Course Instructor

### Relationship and Evidence

Identify the course, term, level, and the instructor's teaching or assessment responsibilities. Establish additional contact through office hours, tutorials, presentations, supervised coursework, or correspondence when supplied.

Useful evidence includes marked assignments, examinations, seminar contributions, questions, feedback responses, and individual contributions to assessed group work. Explain what an assignment tested so the reader can interpret the performance. Preserve the grade scale and any verified cohort comparison.

### Evaluation and Voice

A course instructor can assess conceptual understanding, analytical rigor, written argument, intellectual engagement, preparation, and learning through feedback. Use concrete coursework to explain readiness for advanced study.

Match the scope of the assessment to the contact described. Brief teaching contact can support a focused letter through detailed evidence about an assignment or discussion. Use a clear academic voice that makes the quality of the applicant's reasoning visible.

### Paragraph Pattern

> I taught [applicant] in [course] during [term]. In [assignment], [applicant] [specific analytical action], supporting the argument through [supplied evidence or method]. Following my feedback on [issue], [applicant] [documented revision]. This work demonstrated [evaluative conclusion] and informs my recommendation for [application].

## Several Recommenders and Combined Roles

For several letters, prepare a working comparison of each recommender's relationship, accessible evidence, principal evaluation, and supplied style preferences. Give each letter an emphasis grounded in that relationship.

The same project may appear in several letters when each recommender observed a meaningful aspect of it. Select the details each person can explain and assess. Vary the organization and language around those differences. Use supplied writing samples or stated preferences to guide voice.

For combined roles, establish both relationships and identify the relevant setting when moving between classroom, project, and research evidence. Keep the title of the signatory and the source of each observation accurate when a principal investigator and day-to-day mentor have different involvement.

The paragraph patterns are drafting scaffolds. Fill them from supplied material and adjust the prose to the intended recommender and audience. Essential gaps can remain visibly marked while the rest of the letter is drafted.
